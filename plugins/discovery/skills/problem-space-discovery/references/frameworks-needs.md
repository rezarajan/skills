## 1. Jobs to Be Done

- **Answers**: What are people trying to get done, independent of any product or service?
- **Fits**: Any problem with a human actor trying to make progress.
- **Writes**: `jobs`, linked to `stakeholders`.
- **Sources**: Christensen and colleagues (Competing Against Luck, 2016); Ulwick (Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice, 2016); Moesta and Spiek (jobstobedone.org).

Job types: **functional** (the task), **emotional** (how they want to feel), **social** (how they want to be seen). Strategyzer adds **supporting** jobs: buying, co-creating and transferring value.

Job statement: verb + object + context, with no solution in it.
- Good: "Identify students at risk of chronic absence while there is still time to intervene."
- Bad: "Use the attendance dashboard." (That is a solution.)

Ask about the job by asking about the last time it was done, not about preferences.

**Misuse**: Writing jobs at the wrong altitude. "Run a school" is too broad to analyse; "click export" is too narrow. Aim for the level at which success and failure are visible to the person.

## 2. Universal job map

- **Answers**: Where in the job do people struggle?
- **Source**: Bettencourt and Ulwick, "The Customer-Centered Innovation Map" (HBR, 2008).

Eight steps that most functional jobs pass through:

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| Define | How do they determine goals and plan? |
| Locate | How do they gather the inputs and information needed? |
| Prepare | How do they set up the environment and inputs? |
| Confirm | How do they verify they are ready to proceed? |
| Execute | How do they perform the core task? |
| Monitor | How do they check that it is working? |
| Modify | How do they make adjustments? |
| Conclude | How do they finish, and what follows? |

Mark the steps where pains cluster. Problems often sit in Locate, Confirm and Monitor, not in Execute.

## 3. Desired outcome statements (ODI)

- **Answers**: What measurable criteria do people use to judge how well a job gets done?
- **Fits**: When you need comparable, testable needs, and before ODI scoring.
- **Writes**: `outcome_statement` on pains and gains.
- **Source**: Ulwick, What Customers Want (2005).

Structure: direction of improvement + metric + object of control + contextual clarifier.
- "Minimise the time it takes to identify a student whose attendance is declining."
- "Minimise the likelihood of contacting a family about absence that has already been resolved."

Outcome statements are stable over time and contain no solution. They are the inputs to opportunity scoring.

## 4. Forces of Progress

- **Answers**: Why do people switch to something new, or stay stuck despite pain?
- **Fits**: When adoption, behaviour change or "why don't they use X" is part of the problem.
- **Writes**: `pains` (push), `gains` (pull), `assumptions` and `causes` (anxiety, habit).
- **Source**: Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, the switch interview method (jobstobedone.org).

Four forces:
- **Push** of the current situation (pain with today's way).
- **Pull** of the new solution (attraction of the better state).
- **Anxiety** about the new (risk, effort, uncertainty).
- **Habit** of the present (comfort, inertia, sunk investment).

Change happens only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit. In discovery, anxiety and habit are often the overlooked causes: a problem persists not because nobody wants change, but because the forces holding people back are stronger.

## 5. Value Proposition Canvas: Customer Profile only

- **Answers**: For one segment, what are their jobs, pains and gains, and which matter most?
- **Fits**: Customer-centric problems, after the primary stakeholder is chosen.
- **Writes**: `jobs`, `pains`, `gains` with importance and severity.
- **Source**: Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda and Smith, Value Proposition Design (2014); Strategyzer.

Build only the right-hand side, the Customer Profile. The Value Map (products, pain relievers, gain creators) is solution design and out of scope.

- **Jobs**: functional, social, emotional, supporting. Rank by importance.
- **Pains**: undesired outcomes, obstacles and risks. Rank by severity.
- **Gains**: required (without which nothing works), expected, desired, and unexpected. Rank by relevance.

One profile per segment. Strategyzer stresses that the profile is a set of hypotheses until confirmed with customers; record confidence accordingly.

**Misuse**: Filling every box with plausible guesses. Five evidenced pains beat twenty imagined ones.

## 6. Customer journey and experience maps

- **Answers**: Where does the end-to-end experience break down, including before and after the obvious moment?
- **Fits**: Fragmented experiences, multi-step services, handoffs.
- **Writes**: `pains` with location; `problems` with `where` and `when`.

Columns are stages (including before and after the core interaction). Rows typically include: actions, touchpoints and channels, thoughts and questions, emotional state, pain points, and evidence for each cell. Map the current state, as it happens today. A "future state" map is solution design.

Experience maps are the same idea, not tied to one organisation's service, for understanding a broader human activity.

**Misuse**: Drawing the journey the organisation intends, not the one people actually take. Base it on observation or recounted specific episodes.

## 7. Service blueprint

- **Answers**: Which backstage processes and systems produce the pains customers experience?
- **Fits**: Service and operational problems where front-line symptoms have back-office causes.
- **Writes**: `causes` linking frontstage `pains` to backstage processes; `constraints`.
- **Source**: G. Lynn Shostack, "Designing Services That Deliver" (HBR, 1984).

Layers, top to bottom:
1. Physical evidence (what the customer sees or receives).
2. Customer actions.
3. Line of interaction.
4. Frontstage actions (visible staff or system actions).
5. Line of visibility.
6. Backstage actions (staff actions the customer does not see).
7. Line of internal interaction.
8. Support processes (systems, other teams, vendors).

Mark fail points, wait points and handoffs. Trace each customer-facing pain down the layers to where it originates.

## 8. Empathy map (with caveats)

- **Answers**: What does this person say, think, do and feel in relation to the job?
- **Source**: Dave Gray (XPLANE).
- **Caveat**: Empathy maps are easy to fabricate. Build one only from research data, cite the source of each entry, and keep "thinks" and "feels" entries at low confidence unless directly reported. Prefer the Customer Profile when data is thin.
