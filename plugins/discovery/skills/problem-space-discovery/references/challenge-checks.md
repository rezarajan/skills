# Challenge checks

Run at intake, before upgrading any claim to fact or root cause, and before any synthesis. The checks apply to your own reasoning as much as to the user's.

How to raise a challenge: name the issue, say in one sentence why it matters, and propose a more precise framing or the evidence that would settle it. Raise each issue once; if the user has considered it and given a reason, record the reason and move on.

## 1. Framing checks

- **Solution in disguise**: does the problem statement contain a solution ("we need a dashboard", "lack of training", "no app")? Park the solution in `candidate_solutions` and ask what outcome it was meant to change, for whom.
- **Absence framing**: is the problem phrased as the lack of something? Restate it as the actual negative condition.
- **Whose problem**: who framed this, and what does their role make visible or invisible to them? A manager's framing of staff problems is indirect evidence.
- **Domain mismatch**: is causal analysis being applied to a complex situation, or open-ended exploration to a clear one? Recheck Cynefin.
- **Scope creep or scope starvation**: is the boundary drawn so wide nothing can be done, or so narrow the real cause sits outside it?
- **Symptom as problem**: is the stated problem an observable effect whose causes are unexamined?

## 2. Stakeholder checks

- **Single voice**: does a claim about a group rest on one person or one source?
- **Missing dependents**: who is affected but has no power? Has anyone heard from them directly?
- **Decision-maker gap**: does the person who experiences the problem differ from the one who decides or pays? Is that gap itself part of the problem?
- **Conflicting worldviews**: do stakeholders describe different problems? Record both framings rather than choosing one silently.

## 3. Causal checks

- **Correlation as causation**: is there a mechanism, correct timing, and a comparison case?
- **Single chain**: has a multi-causal problem been reduced to one chain of whys?
- **Blame as cause**: does the chain stop at a person's error without asking what made the error likely?
- **Unexplained IS NOT**: does the proposed cause explain where and when the problem does not occur?
- **Fix history**: have past fixes failed in a way that suggests a systems archetype (fixes that fail, shifting the burden)?

## 4. Evidence checks

- **Assumption as fact**: is any claim stated as fact without supporting evidence about the relevant population?
- **Say as do**: does the evidence consist of opinions or predictions where behaviour is needed?
- **Indirect population**: does secondary research describe a different population, place or time?
- **Confirmation bias**: has anyone looked for disconfirming evidence?
- **Survivorship**: are we only hearing from people still in the system, and not from those who left or gave up?
- **Precision theatre**: are numbers being presented more precisely than their source supports?
- **Importance placement**: does `if_wrong_breaks` actually name the problem statement, primary stakeholder or top problem it claims to break?

## 5. Prioritisation checks

- **Ease as importance**: is something ranked high because it is easy to fix?
- **Confidence hidden in a score**: does a single score blend importance with evidence strength?
- **Opportunity as business case**: is an unmet need being treated as proof that a solution would be adopted, funded or sustainable?
- **Loudness as importance**: is the most-discussed problem the most important, or just the most visible to powerful stakeholders?

## 6. Self-checks for the agent

Before each synthesis, ask:
- Which claims in this summary did I infer rather than receive? Are they labelled as inference?
- Have I applied a framework because it helped, or to fill a section?
- Did I keep an earlier conclusion only to stay consistent?
- Have I quietly started designing a solution?
- Does any probe name a specific intervention instead of the hypothesis it tests?
- Is my confidence language ("clearly", "the root cause") stronger than the evidence?

## 7. The alternative-explanation step

Every synthesis must include:
1. The strongest alternative explanation or framing for the main problem.
2. What evidence would distinguish the two.
3. Which of them the current evidence favours, and how strongly.

If you cannot think of a plausible alternative, say so, and say why. That is itself useful information about how constrained the problem is.
