## 1. Customer Ecosystem Map (Strategyzer)

- **Answers**: Who is involved in, affected by, deciding about or paying for this?
- **Fits**: Any problem with more than one kind of actor. Almost always.
- **Writes**: `stakeholders` with `roles`, and `relationships`.
- **Source**: Strategyzer's Customer Ecosystem Map, used in its playbooks before building customer profiles.

Map these roles, which may be held by the same or different people:
- End users: who directly experiences the process or service.
- Beneficiaries: who gains when it works, even without using it.
- Decision makers: who decides whether anything changes.
- Economic buyers: who pays or controls the budget.
- Channel partners and intermediaries: who delivers, distributes or mediates.
- Also, outside the commercial frame: operators, regulators, influencers, and indirectly affected groups.

Then choose the most important stakeholder for this problem, with a reason, and build the Customer Profile for them first.

**Misuse**: Treating "the user" as a single person. In institutional settings the person who feels the pain is often not the one who decides or pays. That gap is frequently part of the problem.

## 2. Power-Interest grid

- **Answers**: How much influence and engagement does each stakeholder have?
- **Source**: Commonly attributed to Mendelow (1991).

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely |
| **Low power** | Monitor | Keep informed |

In discovery, use it differently from project management: the quadrant tells you whose framing will dominate, and who needs deliberate effort to be heard.

## 3. Stakeholder Salience model

- **Answers**: Whose claims get attention, and why? Who is affected but unheard?
- **Source**: Mitchell, Agle and Wood, "Toward a Theory of Stakeholder Identification and Salience" (Academy of Management Review, 1997).

Three attributes: **power** (ability to impose their will), **legitimacy** (a socially accepted claim), **urgency** (time-sensitivity and criticality of the claim). Combinations give seven types:

| Attributes | Type |
|---|---|
| Power only | Dormant |
| Legitimacy only | Discretionary |
| Urgency only | Demanding |
| Power + legitimacy | Dominant |
| Power + urgency | Dangerous |
| Legitimacy + urgency | Dependent |
| All three | Definitive |

**Dependent** stakeholders matter most in discovery. They have legitimate, urgent claims but no power, so their problems tend to be under-described in the evidence the user already has. They are often students, patients, citizens or front-line staff. Flag them and plan research that reaches them directly.

Salience changes over time; re-check it when the situation shifts.

## 4. CATWOE (Soft Systems Methodology)

- **Answers**: Do stakeholders see fundamentally different problems, and what does each view assume?
- **Fits**: Contested, multi-stakeholder, wicked problems.
- **Writes**: `stakeholders.worldview`; competing `problem_statements` and `alternative_framing`.
- **Source**: Peter Checkland, Soft Systems Methodology (Lancaster University, from the late 1960s; Checkland and Poulter, Learning for Action, 2006).

For each relevant worldview, write a root definition with:
- **C**ustomers: who benefits or suffers from the system.
- **A**ctors: who carries out the activities.
- **T**ransformation: what input is turned into what output.
- **W**eltanschauung: the worldview that makes this transformation meaningful.
- **O**wners: who could stop or change the system.
- **E**nvironmental constraints: what is taken as given.

Two stakeholders with different W will describe different problems from the same facts. Record both. Do not resolve the difference by fiat; make the chosen framing explicit and say whose it is.

## 5. Rich pictures

- **Answers**: What does the whole messy situation look like, including relationships, conflicts and concerns?
- **Fits**: Early in complex, contested situations.
- **Method**: In text environments, describe the picture as a structured list of actors, their concerns (as speech bubbles), relationships, conflicts (crossed swords) and flows. In visual environments, offer a diagram.
- **Source**: Checkland's SSM.

---

## 6. Picking the primary stakeholder

Choose the stakeholder whose problem, if understood well, explains the most about the situation, weighing:
- Who experiences the harm most directly and most often.
- Whose behaviour must change for anything to improve.
- Whose voice is least represented in the evidence so far.
- What the user's mandate covers.

Record the reason. Revisit the choice if research shows the harm sits elsewhere.
