# Context and environment frameworks

Use these to understand the forces around the problem. Apply only the ones relevant to the domain. A public-sector service problem rarely needs Five Forces; a commercial opportunity usually does.

## Contents
1. Business Model Environment Map
2. PESTLE
3. Porter's Five Forces
4. SWOT (with caveats)
5. Horizon scanning
6. Internal organisational context

---

## 1. Business Model Environment Map (Strategyzer)

- **Answers**: Which external forces shape the problem and its opportunities?
- **Fits**: Commercial and quasi-commercial problems; useful for public services with "customers" and providers.
- **Writes**: `constraints`, contextual `assumptions`, `evidence` from research, `questions`.
- **Source**: Osterwalder and Pigneur, Business Model Generation (2010); Strategyzer.

Four areas, each with typical sub-areas:

| Area | Sub-areas |
|---|---|
| Market forces | Market issues, market segments, needs and demands, switching costs, revenue attractiveness |
| Industry forces | Competitors (incumbents), new entrants (insurgents), substitute products and services, stakeholders, suppliers and other value-chain actors |
| Key trends | Technology, regulatory, societal and cultural, socioeconomic |
| Macroeconomic forces | Global market conditions, capital markets, commodities and other resources, economic infrastructure |

For each force recorded, note how it bears on the problem specifically, and its evidence. Drop forces that do not plausibly connect.

## 2. PESTLE

- **Answers**: Which political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors affect the problem?
- **Fits**: Public sector, policy and regulated problems, where the Environment Map's market framing fits poorly.
- **Writes**: `constraints` (especially legal and political), `evidence`, `questions`.

For each factor: what it is, the direction of change, how it affects which stakeholders, and evidence. Separate current factors from expected ones.

**Misuse**: Producing a long generic list. Keep only factors with a plausible causal link to the problem, and say what the link is.

## 3. Porter's Five Forces

- **Answers**: How attractive is this market, and where does power sit in it?
- **Fits**: Opportunity appraisal in competitive markets. Rarely useful for internal or public-service problems.
- **Source**: Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (HBR, 1979); Competitive Strategy (1980).

Forces: rivalry among existing competitors; threat of new entrants; threat of substitutes; bargaining power of buyers; bargaining power of suppliers.

**Misuse**: Using it to justify an opportunity before the underlying need is established. Market attractiveness says nothing about whether the need is real.

---

## 4. SWOT (with caveats)

- **Answers**: A summary of internal strengths and weaknesses against external opportunities and threats.
- **Fits**: Summarising after evidence exists, for audiences who expect it.
- **Misuse**: SWOT invites unevidenced lists. Do not use it as a discovery tool. If you produce one, every entry must point to model nodes with confidence levels. Opportunities in a SWOT are not validated opportunities in this skill's sense.

---

## 5. Horizon scanning

- **Answers**: Which emerging signals could change the problem within the relevant time frame?
- **Fits**: Strategic problems with long horizons; policy.
- **Method**: For each signal, record the source, how strong it is (an isolated report versus a documented trend), its time horizon, and which problem components it would change. Keep signals as `evidence` with low certainty unless backed by trend data.

## 6. Internal organisational context

Not a named framework, but almost always needed for internal problems. Establish:
- Strategic priorities the problem competes with.
- Existing capabilities, processes and systems involved.
- Incentives and performance measures that shape behaviour (these often explain persistent problems; see the systems file).
- Culture and decision rights: who can change what.
- Hard constraints: budget cycles, procurement rules, regulation, staffing.
- History: what has been tried before, and why it stopped.

Record these as `constraints`, `stakeholders` (with incentives) and `causes` where they plausibly sustain the problem.
